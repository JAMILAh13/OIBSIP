## Executive Summary
Social engineering attacks target people rather than technology by exploiting trust, fear, curiosity, urgency and authority. This report examines phishing, pretexting, baiting and quid pro quo attacks, supported by real-world case studies and prevention strategies.

## Introduction
Social engineering targets human behaviour instead of technical vulnerabilities. Attackers manipulate trust, fear and urgency to convince victims to reveal confidential information or perform actions that compromise security.

## Phishing
### Definition
Phishing is a social engineering attack where attackers impersonate trusted organisations or individuals to steal sensitive information.

### Types
- Phishing
- Spear Phishing
- Whaling
- Vishing
- Smishing

### How It Works
1. Impersonate a trusted source.
2. Create urgency.
3. Victim clicks a malicious link.
4. Credentials are stolen.
5. Unauthorised access is gained.

### Case Study
**RSA SecurID (2011):** Attackers used spear phishing emails with malicious attachments to compromise RSA employees.

### Prevention
- Verify senders.
- Enable MFA.
- Avoid suspicious links.
- Report suspicious emails.

## Pretexting
Attackers create a false identity or believable scenario to gain confidential information.

### Case Study
**Twitter (2020):** Employees were socially engineered into providing access to internal administrative tools.

### Prevention
- Verify identities.
- Follow verification procedures.
- Train employees.

## Baiting
Baiting exploits curiosity using infected USB drives or fake downloads.

### Prevention
- Never use unknown USB devices.
- Download software only from trusted websites.
- Restrict removable media.

## Quid Pro Quo
Attackers offer a service in exchange for sensitive information.

## Comparison Table

| Attack | Target | Psychological Lever | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phishing | Users | Fear | Verify emails |
| Spear Phishing | Individual | Trust | MFA |
| Whaling | Executives | Authority | Executive awareness |
| Vishing | Phone users | Trust | Call-back verification |
| Smishing | Mobile users | Urgency | Avoid links |
| Pretexting | Employees | Trust | Identity verification |
| Baiting | Curious users | Curiosity | USB policy |
| Quid Pro Quo | Employees | Reciprocity | Never share credentials |

## Organisational Recommendations
1. Security awareness training.
2. Phishing simulations.
3. Multi-Factor Authentication.
4. Incident reporting procedures.
5. Encourage reporting without fear.

## Conclusion
Organisations should combine technical controls with employee education to reduce the success of social engineering attacks.
